GeoForge is a geometric puzzle web game; not to be confused with "GeoForge, the Godot addon", a terrain generation module that I developed separately and that shares only its name with this project. This one was my storyline project (fil rouge) for the second year of the UHA 4.0.2 programme, in the 2025-2026 academic year, carried out alone from February to April 2026 and defended before the jury. I have re-enrolled in 4.0.2 for 2026-2027, this time with MomoCad as the official storyline project.

## The problem

The 4.0.2 brief asks for an object-oriented application that manages geometric shapes: creating them, composing them, computing their characteristics. One of the suggested paths is a tangram-style game served by a website; I took that path literally and built an arcade puzzle against the clock, in which a target model made of several shapes must be rebuilt on a grid, within a time limit, with a fidelity score.

Because the game is competitive, with a leaderboard, a time limit and stars, the technical problem goes beyond the geometry itself. If the client alone decides that a placement is valid, or computes the score it sends to the server itself, nothing stops a player from tampering with their request to obtain an impossible score. A rule therefore had to be held strictly: the client previews and guides, but only the API decides, and it decides from a state it controls entirely, never from what the client claims to have achieved.

On top of this core, the project also had to work as a small platform: the levels are not only the ones I wrote, players can create their own, and someone has to review them before they are published. This second problem, editorial rather than algorithmic, called for a clear validation workflow rather than a "publish" button with no filter.

## The technical decisions

**The client decides neither whether a placement is valid nor the score: both are computed on the server, by a reproducible method.** In a timed, ranked game, letting the client judge validity or compute the score amounts to letting players mark their own work, and I wanted a reproducible, explainable result rather than an ad hoc grade. `IsPlacementValid` computes the grid cells occupied by a shape from a local mask rotated according to its rotation, and checks for placements outside the grid and for collisions; `AddShape()` and `MoveShape()` automatically roll back any operation that fails this check. The score comes from a fidelity percentage computed by a cell-by-cell comparison between the player's construction and the target model (occupied in both, by the right type of shape, in the right place), rather than from a formula worked out by hand; combined with a time bonus, this percentage feeds the score and its conversion into stars, from one to five. The client only displays a green or red preview, a prediction, never a decision.

**The domain knows neither the database nor HTTP.** I wanted to be able to test all the game logic without starting a server or a database, and to guarantee that a controller cannot contain a business rule that only it would know. `GeoForge.Core`, meaning the `Shape2D` shapes, seven concrete shapes and the `Shape2DFactory` factory, depends on no other project in the solution; `GeoForge.Api` (controllers, DTOs) and `GeoForge.Infrastructure` (EF Core, migrations) depend on it, never the reverse. The 131 xUnit unit tests run on this project alone, with no database or server to start.

**Player-created content follows a multi-state workflow, not a "publish" button.** A badly designed level, too easy, too hard, unplayable, degrades the experience of every other player; publishing without a filter was not acceptable. A level goes through draft, testing by its author, submission, moderation, accepted or refused, with an assigned difficulty, and then publication, all driven from an admin panel that also manages accounts and system levels.

**A level can be generated by an algorithm, and its difficulty assessed automatically, without going through a human each time.** Hand-writing every level of a growing catalogue does not scale, and judging difficulty "by eye" does not give a consistent ranking from one level to the next. Alongside the moderation workflow above, the admin panel exposes a procedural level generator and an automatic assessment of their difficulty, two algorithms that go beyond the scope of a classic CRUD.

**Authentication has to withstand more than a forgotten password.** A system of accounts and a leaderboard is a natural target for automated login attempts if nothing slows them down. It relies on JWT tokens, passwords hashed with BCrypt and roles that protect the sensitive routes; after three failed logins, an account is locked for fifteen minutes before another attempt is allowed.

**Phaser stayed an engine walled off in its own corner, not mixed in with the rest of React.** A Phaser canvas mounted and unmounted in step with React's lifecycle, through re-renders and navigation between pages, easily creates ghost instances of the game that keep running in the background. The game's scenes (`GameScene`, `ShapeRenderer`) live behind a stable boundary, and the state of the current game is mirrored in the Zustand store `gameStore`: the rest of the interface, meaning the displayed timer, the buttons and the modals, never needs to read the inside of the canvas to know where the game stands.

## What I learned from it

That the rule "the client decides nothing" is simpler to put in place from the first controller than to add after the fact: because placement validation and scoring were on the server from the start, I never had to go back to close a cheating loophole. That integrating a game engine into a web application is not a mere wiring detail: Phaser and React each have their own idea of who owns the display, and the only way to avoid conflicts is to decide explicitly, once, where the boundary runs, rather than redraw it at every bug. That documenting an algorithm before the defence forces you to check that you truly understand it yourself, not just that it compiles and passes the tests. And that setting myself a written framework for the use of AI, with the explicit goal of being able to re-explain and redo everything without help, changed the way I work more than any architectural choice.
